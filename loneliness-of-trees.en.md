There are many lone trees in many places on Earth. An Internet search of the term shows many photographs. Most of them are of singular trees across a variety of landscapes: lakes, mountains, rivers and fields[^1]. Moreover, I know that there are many lone trees as I have perceived them. However, unlike the search engine algorithm I have also seen lone trees across the urban landscapes of modern cities. There is a qualitative difference between a lone tree known by the search algorithm and the other one, that I know. And perhaps it is already telling that the algorithm does not show me the lone urban trees. After all, these algorithms only show me what most of us want to see. Somehow, it appears, the urban tree is not as aesthetically alluring, as often sought as a symbol. Nevertheless, there are indeed many lone trees, at least if by lone we mean singular. But the loneliness of the trees runs deeper.

A modern biological theory uncovers the relationship between mycorrhizal networks and trees’ roots. Accordingly, the symbiotic relationship allows for emergence of communication, learning and memory, or more generally complex adaptive behavior. What appears clear is that from an evolutionary perspective the relationship between the trees and mycorrhizal networks is, in most cases, mutually positive. Moreover, the transfer of information between the trees sharing a network occurs, as ‘carbon, phosphorus, nitrogen, water, defence compounds, and allelochemicals’[^2], are exchanged[^3].

Clearly, the urban trees are deprived of something. For one, distances between trees in cities is in most cases significant enough to limit the positive effects of network participation. Moreover, the mycorrhizal networks themselves, do not appear to thrive in the urban environment. And so there is a sort of a loneliness of the trees that is, as is the case in more anthropomorphised bodies, manifested both in the visible, as well as in the hidden. But it appears that this loneliness remains unacknowledged by most. Indeed, oftentimes a lone urban tree is not aesthetic enough to draw our attention, except maybe for the moments when it flowers. In fact, the tree belongs to a different order than the majority of the surrounding urban environment; it abides by different rules. The scale at which man and the tree function is different as well and while man certainly is capable of understanding the slower, more cyclical and static scale of the tree, it requires of him a certain inclination and effort. There is conflict between the roots and the concrete, between the branches and the electricity lines, between the trunks and the future development plans. The ancient days, when holy trees were protected by custom are gone; the modern law does little to protect the urban trees. Most conflicts are resolved in favor of the concrete and the trees that remain become more and more lonely.

Man is no doubt affected by this loneliness physically. His health deteriorates. The air he breaths becomes unclean. His mental capacities dwindle. Lone trees are not enough to save man, lacking the advice of their old-growth ancestors. All this happens because of the similarity emergent from coevolution of man and tree, who are intertwined in a mutualistic multiplicity. Man’s life is not possible without the oxygen exhaled by the tree. Tree’s life is not possible without the carbon dioxide produced by man. A rhizome[^4] connects man with the tree with the fungi with the forest. Together they guarantee each other's continuation and what affects one must necessarily affect the other[^5].

As much is man made of man cells as of others (archaea, bacteria, fungi) and by no means are the others antagonists. Instead, they make an integral part of the man organism. A testament to the intertwining of archaea-bacteria-fungi and man is that the gut microbiota (strictly non-man cells) is responsible for the synthesis of around 95% of serotonin regulating, among others, mood, appetite and sleep[^6]. Man is then already a cyborg composite made out of many mutualistic organisms, an analogous mutualism to that between the tree and the fungi or man and the tree.

The main difference between man-tree and man-archaea-bacteria-fungi mutualisms is that the former arises *ex vivo* and the latter *in vivo*. Nevertheless, man without serotonin is as much not-man as man without air and so if one admits the man-archaea-bacteria-fungi organism one must necessarily also admit man-tree. Applying transitivity yields a tree-man-archaea-bacteria-fungi complex and mycorrhizal networks attach another link completing the circle (as mushrooms are known to do). Still, the archaea-bacteria-fungi are all conveniently within man, carried at all times in the gut or on the skin. The tree (as the forest), on the other hand, is the one within which man is carried. Man is then akin to a matryoshka, made of a progression of organisms hidden within one another[^7].

Both in the context of trees and the archaea-bacteria-fungi the mutualism constitutes a man-sustaining relation, one that assures man’s material existence. Also those, who would question the interpretation of man as a matryoshka made of many organisms, would naturally give more attention to the objects that are sustaining man. As long as man accepts his fate and does not strive to destroy himself he must at least acknowledge the objects without whose intercession he is lost. The disinclination to acknowledge the lone trees and their continuous disappearance reflects man’s inner auto-destructiveness. It is not due to man’s evil intentions, that he does not acknowledge them, but forgetfulness, alike the one of Orpheus.

Eurydice, a nymph, dies bitten by a snake. The dryads and forests wail for their sister. Mourning her along with them, Orpheus adds his voice to the requiem. Understanding the loneliness that awaits him without the nymph he accepts his own mortality and descends into the underworld, where the roots and fungi meet in mycorrhizal networks. There with his art he sways the gods, who agree to return his beloved forest spirit. He must lead her out, her following in his footsteps, and not look behind until they are on the other side. Virgil reports a sudden madness seizing Orpheus. Forgetful and impassioned he looks around. According to Ovid, he turns his eyes because he is afraid Eurydice is not there anymore. Maybe Orpheus is himself already beyond the threshold and so his perspective allows him to turn but he forgets Eurydice might still be in the land of the dead. He forgets her because remembering her makes him lonely and causes him suffering. As long as she remains dead (in the underworld) he cannot think of her without pain of loneliness (thus the only way to save her is to accept the pain - the possibility of losing).

And so why does man make trees lonely in the first place, why does he lose them? After all, if the trees had not been made lonely, it would not have been necessary to forget them. As with Orpheus, he is blinded by the future attainment, his perspective moves into the future and so he acts as if it is already now. As a lens of a camera focuses on the detail the background blurs, so man envelops himself in the details allowing the background to fade away. And yet the background is always there, always indivisible from the act of perception. Orpheus does not move on his way without accessing this detailed perception that synthesizes a dismembered, practical and graspable object. He moves as if haunted by it, possessed by a singular aim, anchoring his whole being to the future attainment. No evil appears in itself in an ability to focus selectively, but there awaits in it an ancient danger of getting lost in the aim and the means to achieve it. Orpheus objectifies Eurydice, man objectifies the forest as a resource and builds an industrial new world order out of its burning wood. They both become lost in the world of their own making (of quantities; present means and future ends) that begets a reductionist illusion of understanding[^8].

Finally, in his arrogance, man assumes he is able to account for the background, for the far greater complexity, hidden behind a veil. Man not only claims he knows his aim, but further, he knows how to reach it in the differential currents of the phenomenon. The teleological mode of being, apparently necessary for attainment, is made into the very reason, which renders the aim unattainable. In the underworld, Orpheus is a part of the mystical sphere and so he acknowledges the mystery, the blurred area in the corner of his eye, he does not need to turn as he trusts that Eurydice awaits him in that blurred area. The awareness of ignorance stops him from slipping into dissecting reality. As soon as he crosses the threshold, reunited with the world, he claims to know all again, complacently accustomed, he does not follow the rule, which does not make sense anymore. And so as Orpheus loses his dryad so man, in the growing alienation of modern life, loses his forest and becomes lonely himself. To be with trees he goes to the other world, to the last remaining pockets of sylvan resistance. But each time he returns to his world he falls back into his misguided ways.

Man does not ponder the loneliness of trees, but the loneliness of the trees is his nevertheless. Orpheus looks back to perceive and man turns away not to, yet their motivations and fate are alike. Ultimately, man cannot escape perceiving the tree and even if all trees were removed from him as Eurydice was from Orpheus, he would still be haunted by the memory (or suffocate[^9]). Man makes himself lonely through the tree, exposing himself constantly to the deep loneliness buried in the blurred background that at last becomes sharp. Eurydice screams, Orpheus’ eyes widen in recognition and it is always already too late.
